We now move to **Step 2: analytical fixed‑point and stability analysis**, with the goal of deriving conditions for bistability and the role of permeability. The full five‑dimensional system is analytically intractable in closed form because of the sigmoids, but we can make significant progress using two standard techniques:

1. **Slow–fast decomposition** – since \(\rho_P = 0.02\) is much smaller than the other rates, we treat \(P\) as a slow parameter and analyse the four‑dimensional fast subsystem \((U,B,T,E)\) for each fixed \(P\).

2. **Steep‑sigmoid approximation** – the sharpness parameters \(k_B,k_E,k_P \sim 20\) mean the sigmoids act nearly as switches. This allows us to partition the phase space into regimes and solve the resulting piecewise‑linear equations explicitly.

We will derive the fixed points of the fast subsystem in the two extreme regimes that correspond to the open and closed attractors, and then show how the slow \(P\) dynamics selects between them.

---

## 1. Fast subsystem: \(P\) held fixed

We define the fast subsystem as \((U,B,T,E)\) with \(P\) a parameter. The equations are those given earlier, with \(P\) frozen in the expression \(Q = (1-P)B\).

Because \(k_B,k_E\) are large, we replace the sigmoids by step functions:

\[
\sigma\bigl(k_B(F-\theta)\bigr) \approx
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } F < \theta, \\
1 & \text{if } F > \theta,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\sigma\bigl(k_E(\frac{\alpha U}{1+\eta Q}-c_E)\bigr) \approx
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } \frac{\alpha U}{1+\eta Q} < c_E, \\
1 & \text{if } \frac{\alpha U}{1+\eta Q} > c_E.
\end{cases}
\]

This is a standard approximation for bistable systems with sharp thresholds.

### Regime I: “Open” equilibrium (low \(B\), high \(T,E\))

Assume \(F < \theta\), so boundary drive is off. Then

\[
\dot B = -d_B B \quad \Longrightarrow \quad B \to 0.
\]

With \(B=0\), we have \(Q=0\). The remaining equations become

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U &= n(1-U) - \alpha E U,\\
\dot T &= \rho_T E - d_T T,\\
\dot E &= \rho_E \,\mathbf{1}_{\{\alpha U > c_E\}} - d_E E.
\end{aligned}
\]

For consistency, if \(E\) is positive, then \(U\) must satisfy \(\alpha U > c_E\) so that exploration drive is on. Setting \(\dot U=0\) and \(\dot E=0\) with \(E\)-drive on (\(E = \rho_E/d_E\) but bounded by 1; here \(\rho_E/d_E = 0.073/0.059 \approx 1.24\), so \(E=1\)), we get

\[
n(1-U) = \alpha U \quad \Longrightarrow \quad U = \frac{n}{n+\alpha}.
\]

With the default parameters,

\[
U_{\text{open}} = \frac{0.12}{0.12+1.339} \approx 0.082.
\]

But \(\alpha U_{\text{open}} \approx 1.339 \times 0.082 \approx 0.110\), which is **less than** \(c_E = 0.489\). Thus the exploration drive would be off, contradicting our assumption. Therefore the open equilibrium cannot have \(E\)-drive on; instead, \(E\) must decay to 0, which then pushes \(U\to 1\), which would turn the drive on. The step approximation fails to capture the smooth partial activation that actually occurs in the simulations. The true open equilibrium has a small but nonzero \(E\) and \(B\), which we saw numerically (\(B\approx 0.147\), \(E\approx 0.179\)). This is because the sigmoids are not perfect switches; they allow partial activation.

Thus a purely step‑function analysis is too crude for the open regime. We can instead perform a **linear stability analysis around the numerically found open equilibrium** to understand its stability, rather than trying to derive its location analytically.

### Regime II: “Closed” equilibrium (high \(B\), low \(T,E\))

Assume \(F > \theta\), so boundary drive is on, and assume the exploration drive is off: \(\frac{\alpha U}{1+\eta Q} < c_E\). With \(E\)-drive off, \(E\to 0\). Then \(T\to 0\) (since \(\dot T = -\dots\)). Boundary equation: \(\dot B = \rho_B - d_B B\), so \(B \to \rho_B/d_B \approx 0.154/0.117 \approx 1.316\), but bounded at \(B=1\). Hence the closed equilibrium is approximately

\[
B \approx 1,\quad T \approx 0,\quad E \approx 0,\quad U \approx 1.
\]

This matches the numerical closed state. The felt uncertainty at this equilibrium is

\[
F_{\text{closed}} = \frac{s \cdot 1}{(1+\lambda\cdot 0)(1+\mu\cdot 1)} = \frac{s}{1+\mu}.
\]

With \(s=1.5\) and \(\mu=1.841\), \(F_{\text{closed}} \approx 0.528\). Since \(\theta = 0.196\), we indeed have \(F_{\text{closed}} > \theta\), so the boundary drive remains on. Also, for the exploration drive to be off we need \(\frac{\alpha U}{1+\eta Q} = \frac{\alpha}{1+\eta (1-P) \cdot 1} < c_E\), which holds for most \(P\) (e.g., \(P=0\) gives \(\alpha/(1+\eta)=1.339/3.065=0.437 < 0.489\); for higher \(P\) it becomes even smaller because \(Q\) decreases). So the closed equilibrium is self‑consistent.

The stability of this closed equilibrium can be checked by linearising the fast subsystem around it. Because \(B=1\) saturates, we treat \(B\) as fixed at 1 for the local analysis of the other variables. Then \(Q = 1-P\), a constant. The remaining equations for \(U,T,E\) become linear:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\dot U &= n(1-U) - \alpha E (1-\beta(1-P)) U,\\
\dot T &= \rho_T E (1-\beta_T(1-P)) - d_T T - \gamma(1-P) T,\\
\dot E &= \rho_E \sigma(\dots) - d_E E.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since the exploration drive is off in the closed state, the term \(\sigma(\cdot)\) is approximately 0 (or very small), so \(E\) decays. The Jacobian is lower‑triangular-ish, and we can see that the eigenvalues are negative (decay terms dominate), so the closed equilibrium is stable.

## 2. Bistability conditions

The existence of two stable equilibria requires that the open equilibrium is stable and the closed equilibrium is stable for the **same** parameter values and the **same** fixed \(P\). We already know from simulations that for a range of \(P\) (or when \(P\) is allowed to adapt) both attractors exist.

For a given \(P\), the fast subsystem can have:

- an **open equilibrium** with small \(B\), moderate \(T,E\);
- a **closed equilibrium** with \(B=1\), \(T,E\approx0\).

The open equilibrium stability depends on the balance between exploration, trust, and boundary suppression. In the open state, \(B\) is small but nonzero; \(P\) is moderate to high, so \(Q=(1-P)B\) is small. The exploration drive is partially active because \(U\) is moderate, and trust is high, keeping felt uncertainty low.

A necessary condition for the open equilibrium to exist and be stable is that the effective boundary suppression \(Q\) is small enough not to crush \(T\) and \(E\). That is, we need

\[
(1-P)B_{\text{open}} \ll 1.
\]

From simulations, \(B_{\text{open}} \approx 0.147\), \(P_{\text{open}} \approx 0.70\), so \(Q \approx 0.044\), which is indeed small.

The closed equilibrium exists if the felt uncertainty with \(B=1\) exceeds \(\theta\), i.e.

\[
\frac{s}{1+\mu} > \theta.
\]

With the default parameters, this holds for \(s > \theta(1+\mu) \approx 0.196 \times 2.841 \approx 0.557\). Since our base \(s=0.908\) and in many experiments \(s\ge1.5\), the closed equilibrium always exists for those \(s\).

Thus **bistability occurs when \(s\) is above the closed‑equilibrium threshold (so closed exists) and simultaneously the open equilibrium is stable (which requires the exploration–trust loop to be strong enough to keep \(B\) low)**. The open equilibrium loses stability when \(s\) becomes too large or when \(P\) is too low, leading to the collapse we saw.

---

## 3. Role of the slow permeability \(P\)

The full five‑dimensional system adds the slow equation for \(P\):

\[
\dot P = \rho_P \left( 1 - \sigma\bigl(k_P(F-\theta_P)\bigr) - P \right).
\]

In the open equilibrium, \(F\) is low (because \(B\) is small and \(T\) is high), typically below \(\theta_P=0.15\). Hence the term \(1-\sigma(\cdot)\) is near 1, so \(P\) tends to 1. In the closed equilibrium, \(F \approx s/(1+\mu) > 0.15\), so the term \(1-\sigma(\cdot)\) is near 0, and \(P\) tends to 0.

Thus the slow dynamics create a **positive feedback**: open states maintain high permeability, which in turn keeps \(Q\) low and reinforces openness; closed states drive permeability down, making boundaries more damaging and reinforcing closure. This feedback is what produces the wide hysteresis: once the system falls into the closed equilibrium, \(P\) drops, making it even harder to escape.

The system can be viewed as moving on the **slow manifold** of the fast subsystem equilibria, with \(P\) as the slow coordinate. The open and closed branches of the fast‑subsystem equilibrium manifold are separated by a saddle; the slow \(P\) dynamics carries the system along the branch it is on. A sufficiently large shock to \(s\) or a forced drop in \(P\) can push the system across the separatrix, causing a transition between branches. The hysteresis width is determined by the distance between the branch points where the open and closed equilibria lose stability.

---

## 4. Summary of analytical findings

- The closed equilibrium is simple and always exists when \(s/(1+\mu) > \theta\).
- The open equilibrium is a low‑\(B\), moderate‑\(T,E\) state that exists only when the exploration–trust feedback is strong enough to keep felt uncertainty below the boundary threshold. Its stability depends critically on \(P\): high \(P\) keeps \(Q\) small and preserves trust, while low \(P\) destroys trust and makes the open state unstable.
- Bistability arises from the coexistence of these two stable states for a range of \(s\) and \(P\).
- The slow permeability dynamics reinforces whichever state the system currently occupies, creating hysteresis and path dependence.

This analysis, while not a full bifurcation proof, gives a clear mechanistic picture of why the model behaves as observed. The next step is to use these insights to design **preregistered predictions** and then test them with systematic simulations over the full parameter space.
